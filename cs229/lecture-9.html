Lecture 9

When the Gaussian flattens

\(n \ll d\), a covariance matrix that collapses, and the model that keeps the correlations without paying for all of them.

Pratyush · CS229 · factor analysis · latent variables · EM

Fitting a single Gaussian is the easiest thing in the course. Two closed-form estimators:

\[ \mu = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{(i)}, \qquad \Sigma = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x^{(i)}-\mu)(x^{(i)}-\mu)^T \]

and you're done. This works whenever the training set is comfortably larger than the dimension it lives in. When it isn't, it doesn't fail gracefully — it fails completely.

1Why it collapses

With \(n\) points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(n < d+1\), the data spans at most an \((n-1)\)-dimensional affine subspace. There are directions in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) along which your data has exactly zero observed spread — not "little", zero. So the MLE assigns them zero variance, and \(\Sigma\) is singular.

Not a numerical problem\(\Sigma\) being singular is the correct maximum-likelihood answer. The question was badly posed. Regularising the arithmetic doesn't fix a question with no content.

Now look at what the density needs:

\[ p(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\lvert\Sigma\rvert^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\tfrac12 (x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right) \]

\(\Sigma^{-1}\) doesn't exist and \(\lvert\Sigma\rvert^{1/2} = 0\), so the normaliser is \(1/0\). The fitted Gaussian has put all its probability mass on the affine hull of the data. It has flattened onto the subspace and has no thickness at all.

2Two fixes that don't work

The standard move is to restrict the space of \(\Sigma\) you'll consider.

Diagonal \(\Sigma\). Fit only \(\Sigma_{jj} = \frac1n\sum_i (x_j^{(i)} - \mu_j)^2\), the per-coordinate variance. Contours become axis-aligned ellipses. Non-singular for \(n \ge 2\).

\(\Sigma = \sigma^2 I\). One number, \(\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{nd}\sum_j\sum_i (x_j^{(i)}-\mu_j)^2\). Contours become spheres. Also fine for \(n\ge2\).

Both are non-singular. Both are useless for the thing you wanted. A diagonal \(\Sigma\) asserts that the coordinates are uncorrelated and independent — you came looking for covariance structure, and the repair was to assume there isn't any.

So the question sharpens. Can you keep some correlation structure without paying for a full \(d\times d\) matrix you can't afford?

3Digression: slicing a Gaussian

Partition a jointly Gaussian vector, \(x_1\in\mathbb{R}^r\), \(x_2\in\mathbb{R}^s\):

\[ x = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2\end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12}\\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right) \]

Marginal: \(x_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_{11})\). You just read the block off. Marginalising a Gaussian is deletion.

Conditional: \(x_1\mid x_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{1|2}, \Sigma_{1|2})\) with

\[ \mu_{1|2} = \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2), \qquad \Sigma_{1|2} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \]

Look at what's missing\(\Sigma_{1|2}\) has no \(x_2\) in it. Observing \(x_2\) shrinks your uncertainty by a fixed amount no matter what value you observed. Peculiar to Gaussians, and worth not glossing over.

The conditional mean is the prior mean plus a correction term — literally a regression of \(x_1\) onto the observed deviation of \(x_2\), with \(\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\) playing the role of regression coefficients. And the conditional covariance is the prior covariance minus something PSD: conditioning can only reduce uncertainty.

Park these two formulas. They turn out to be the entire E-step.

4The model

Introduce a latent \(z\in\mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k < d\):

\[ z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad x\mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu + \Lambda z,\; \Psi) \]

with parameters \(\mu\in\mathbb{R}^d\), \(\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\), and \(\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) diagonal. Equivalently, and more readably:

\[ x = \mu + \Lambda z + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Psi),\quad z\perp\epsilon \]

The generative story in one sentence: draw a point in a low-dimensional space, map it affinely into \(\mathbb{R}^d\), then blur it with independent per-coordinate noise. The data lies near a \(k\)-dimensional plane sitting inside \(d\)-space, and everything off that plane is noise.

Which is a much better description of the \(n \ll d\) situation than "flat Gaussian" was. You genuinely don't have enough points to pin down a shape in \(d\) dimensions — so model the belief that the shape only has \(k\) dimensions.

What distribution does this actually define?

\(z\) and \(x\) are jointly Gaussian, so we only need means and blocks. \(\mathbb{E}[z]=0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[x] = \mu + \Lambda\mathbb{E}[z] + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = \mu\). For the blocks:

\[ \Sigma_{zz} = I, \qquad \Sigma_{zx} = \mathbb{E}[z(\Lambda z + \epsilon)^T] = \mathbb{E}[zz^T]\Lambda^T + \mathbb{E}[z\epsilon^T] = \Lambda^T \]

(the cross term dies by independence and zero mean), and

\[ \Sigma_{xx} = \mathbb{E}[(\Lambda z + \epsilon)(\Lambda z + \epsilon)^T] = \Lambda\mathbb{E}[zz^T]\Lambda^T + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon\epsilon^T] = \Lambda\Lambda^T + \Psi \] \[ \begin{bmatrix} z\\x\end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}\vec{0}\\\mu\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} I & \Lambda^T\\ \Lambda & \Lambda\Lambda^T+\Psi\end{bmatrix}\right) \]

Marginalise and there it is:

\[ x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\; \Lambda\Lambda^T + \Psi) \]

Why it never collapses\(\Lambda\Lambda^T\) is singular by construction (rank \(\le k < d\)). Adding a strictly positive diagonal \(\Psi\) is exactly what restores full rank — the noise floor is the fix.

So factor analysis is a structural claim about \(\Sigma\): low rank plus diagonal. Put the three options side by side and it's clearly the right middle ground.

ModelParameters in ΣCorrelations
Full \(\Sigma\)\(d(d+1)/2\)all of them — needs \(n \ge d+1\)
Diagonal \(\Sigma\)\(d\)none
\(\Lambda\Lambda^T + \Psi\)\(dk + d\)confined to a \(k\)-dim subspace

5Fitting it: EM again

The log-likelihood is a Gaussian density in \(\Lambda\Lambda^T+\Psi\). Try to maximise it directly and you get nowhere — no closed form is known. But \(z\) is latent and the model is exactly the shape Lecture 8 handles, so: EM.

E-step

We need \(Q_i(z^{(i)}) = p(z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};\mu,\Lambda,\Psi)\). We already have the joint. Substitute its blocks into the conditional formulas from §3 and the E-step falls out with no work:

\[ \mu_{z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}} = \Lambda^T(\Lambda\Lambda^T+\Psi)^{-1}(x^{(i)}-\mu) \] \[ \Sigma_{z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}} = I - \Lambda^T(\Lambda\Lambda^T+\Psi)^{-1}\Lambda \]

That's why the digression was there. The posterior over the latent factors is just a Gaussian conditional, and conditioning Gaussians is a formula.

M-step for \(\Lambda\)

Maximise \(\sum_i \mathbb{E}_{z\sim Q_i}[\log p(x^{(i)}\mid z^{(i)};\mu,\Lambda,\Psi)]\) over \(\Lambda\) — drop everything not depending on it, take the gradient, set to zero:

\[ \Lambda = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x^{(i)}-\mu)\,\mathbb{E}\big[z^{(i)T}\big]\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[z^{(i)}z^{(i)T}\big]\right)^{-1} \]

Recognise it\(\theta^T = (y^TX)(X^TX)^{-1}\). It's the normal equation with \(z\) in the role of the design matrix — because \(x\) really is a linear function of \(z\) plus noise, and given guesses for \(z\), finding \(\Lambda\) is regression.

Which should look extremely familiar. It's least squares, fitting the unknown linear map \(\Lambda\) from the latent \(z\)'s to the observed \(x\)'s.

6The trap

Now evaluate those two expectations under \(Q_i = \mathcal{N}(\mu_{z|x}, \Sigma_{z|x})\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[z^{(i)T}] = \mu_{z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}}^T, \qquad \mathbb{E}[z^{(i)}z^{(i)T}] = \mu_{z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}}\mu_{z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}}^T + \Sigma_{z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}} \]

The second one uses \(\mathbb{E}[YY^T] = \mathbb{E}[Y]\mathbb{E}[Y]^T + \mathrm{Cov}(Y)\). Substituting:

\[ \Lambda = \left(\sum_i (x^{(i)}-\mu)\mu_{z^{(i)}|x^{(i)}}^T\right)\left(\sum_i \mu_{z^{(i)}|x^{(i)}}\mu_{z^{(i)}|x^{(i)}}^T + \Sigma_{z^{(i)}|x^{(i)}}\right)^{-1} \]

The mistake to not makeCompute \(\mathbb{E}[z]\) in the E-step, plug it in wherever \(z\) appears in the M-step. It works for mixtures of Gaussians. It is wrong here.

That \(\Sigma_{z|x}\) sitting inside the inverse is the whole point of this section. \(\mathbb{E}[zz^T] \ne \mathbb{E}[z]\mathbb{E}[z]^T\) — they differ by exactly the posterior covariance.

So if you'd treated the E-step as producing a best guess \(\hat z^{(i)}\) and then run ordinary least squares on those guesses, you'd be doing regression while pretending you know \(z\) exactly. You don't. The posterior is a distribution with real width, and the M-step has to carry that width.

The E-step returns a distribution, not a point. The M-step maximises an expectation under that distribution — so whichever moments the objective needs, all of them come along. Collapse \(Q\) to its mean and you're no longer running EM.

The remaining updates are anticlimactic by comparison. \(\mu = \frac1n\sum_i x^{(i)}\) — and note it doesn't depend on \(Q_i\) at all, so compute it once and never touch it again. \(\Psi\) is the diagonal of

\[ \Phi = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(x^{(i)}x^{(i)T} - x^{(i)}\mu_{z|x}^T\Lambda^T - \Lambda\mu_{z|x}x^{(i)T} + \Lambda\big(\mu_{z|x}\mu_{z|x}^T + \Sigma_{z|x}\big)\Lambda^T\Big) \]

with \(\Psi_{ii} = \Phi_{ii}\) and everything off-diagonal thrown away, because \(\Psi\) was diagonal by assumption.

The reframe

The instinct with \(n \ll d\) is to treat it as a shortage — not enough data, patch the estimator, move on. Factor analysis treats it as information. If \(n\) points can't determine a shape in \(d\) dimensions, that's a reason to believe the shape doesn't have \(d\) dimensions.

So you write that belief into the model: \(k\) real directions, and independent noise on top. The singular covariance stops being a failure to be worked around and becomes the structure you were modelling all along.
