Lecture 11

The rotation you can't see

ICA finds a new basis, same as PCA. But it needs the data to be non-Gaussian — and that requirement isn't a limitation, it's the entire resource the method runs on.

Pratyush · CS229 · ICA · cocktail party · change of variables

Structurally ICA looks like PCA: given data, produce a new basis to represent it in. The goals share nothing.

Not dimension reductionPCA discards directions and hands back \(k < d\) coordinates. ICA keeps all \(d\) and just re-picks which \(d\) they are.

PCA wants the directions carrying the most variance, and throws the rest away. ICA wants directions that are statistically independent, and keeps every one of them. Nothing is compressed.

The cocktail party problem. \(d\) people talking at once, \(d\) microphones scattered around the room. Each microphone sits at a different distance from each speaker, so each records a different linear blend of the same \(d\) voices. Can you unscramble them?

\[ x = As \]

\(s^{(i)}_j\) is what speaker \(j\) said at time \(i\); \(x^{(i)}_j\) is what microphone \(j\) recorded at time \(i\); \(A\) is the unknown square mixing matrix. The goal is the unmixing matrix \(W = A^{-1}\), so that

\[ s^{(i)} = Wx^{(i)}, \qquad s^{(i)}_j = w_j^Tx^{(i)} \]

with \(w_j^T\) the \(j\)-th row of \(W\). Each row is a recipe for cancelling everyone except one speaker.

1What you can never recover

Two ambiguities are baked in, and both are harmless.

Permutation. For any permutation matrix \(P\), the data can't distinguish \(W\) from \(PW\). You recover the set of sources but not their labelling — there's no such thing as "speaker 1". Nobody cares.

Scale. Replace \(A\) by \(2A\) and every \(s^{(i)}\) by \(0.5\,s^{(i)}\), and \(x\) is bit-for-bit identical. More generally, scale a column of \(A\) by \(\alpha\) and its source by \(1/\alpha\). So you cannot recover a source's amplitude — but amplitude is volume, and sign flips are inaudible (\(s\) and \(-s\) sound the same through a speaker). Nobody cares here either.

Those are annoyances. The next one is an obstruction.

2Why Gaussians break it

Take \(d=2\) and \(s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). The contours of \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) are circles — the density is rotationally symmetric. Hold that thought.

Observe \(x = As\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[x] = A\mathbb{E}[s] = 0\) and

\[ \mathrm{Cov}[x] = \mathbb{E}[Ass^TA^T] = A\,\mathrm{Cov}[s]\,A^T = AA^T \]

so \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, AA^T)\). Now let \(R\) be any orthogonal matrix, \(RR^T = R^TR = I\), and set \(A' = AR\). If the mixing had been \(A'\) instead:

\[ \mathbb{E}[x'x'^T] = \mathbb{E}[ARss^T(AR)^T] = AR\,I\,R^TA^T = AA^T \]

Second moments are rotation-blindA Gaussian is fully determined by its covariance, and covariance cannot tell \(A\) from \(AR\). There is nothing else to look at.

The same distribution. Not approximately — exactly. \(A\) and \(AR\) generate observations you could never tell apart, for every orthogonal \(R\). There's a whole rotation group's worth of mixing matrices consistent with your data, and no amount of it will narrow them down.

Gaussian sources \(\Rightarrow\) PCA is all you can have. Non-Gaussian sources \(\Rightarrow\) ICA becomes possible.

That split is the cleanest way to hold the two methods apart. PCA only ever consults second moments; it whitens the data and then has nothing left to say, because after whitening every rotation looks the same. ICA's whole job is to spend that leftover rotational freedom — and the only information capable of spending it lives beyond second moments. Which is to say, in the non-Gaussianity.

So the assumption "the sources are not Gaussian" isn't a technical caveat you'd remove if you could. Remove it and there is nothing to find.

3Digression: what a linear map does to a density

Before the algorithm, one piece of machinery. \(s\) has density \(p_s\), and \(x = As\). What's \(p_x\)?

The tempting answer is \(p_x(x) = p_s(Wx)\): invert to find which \(s\) produced this \(x\), read off its density. It's wrong, and one line kills it. Let \(s\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]\) and \(A = 2\). Then \(x\) is uniform on \([0,2]\), so \(p_x(x) = 0.5\) there. But \(p_s(Wx) = p_s(0.5x) = 1\) on that interval. Off by a factor of \(0.5 = |W|\).

\[ \boxed{\;p_x(x) = p_s(Wx)\cdot|W|\;}, \qquad W = A^{-1} \]

That's the memorable version: \(A\) maps the unit hypercube to a region of volume \(|A|\). Probability is conserved, volume isn't, so the density picks up the reciprocal factor. It generalises the uniform example exactly.

4The algorithm

Write down the independence assumption — this is "independent components", stated:

\[ p(s) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} p_s(s_j) \]

Modelling the joint as a product of marginals is precisely the claim that the sources don't interact. Push it through the change of variables:

\[ p(x) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} p_s(w_j^Tx)\cdot|W| \]

All that's left is choosing \(p_s\). Easiest route is through a cdf, since a cdf just has to be monotone from 0 to 1 — and the density is its derivative. The Gaussian cdf is off the table by §2. A reasonable default that rises slowly from 0 to 1 is the sigmoid \(g(s) = 1/(1+e^{-s})\), so \(p_s = g'\). (Substitute something better if you actually know the source distribution.)

\[ \ell(W) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log g'(w_j^Tx^{(i)}) + \log|W|\right) \]

What \(\log|W|\) is doing\(g'\) peaks at 0, so the first term alone is maximised by \(W = 0\) — every source silenced. \(\log|W| \to -\infty\) as \(W\) goes singular. The Jacobian term is what keeps the answer from collapsing.

Worth pausing on that determinant. It arrived as bookkeeping from §3, but it's load-bearing: without it, the likelihood is maximised by shrinking \(W\) to zero. The change-of-variables factor is the only thing pushing \(W\) away from degeneracy.

Differentiate, using \(\nabla_W|W| = |W|(W^{-1})^T\), and you get stochastic gradient ascent:

Bell–Sejnowski, as MLE \[ W := W + \alpha\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1-2g(w_1^Tx^{(i)}) \\ 1-2g(w_2^Tx^{(i)}) \\ \vdots \\ 1-2g(w_d^Tx^{(i)})\end{bmatrix} x^{(i)T} + (W^T)^{-1}\right) \]

On convergence, recover the sources with \(s^{(i)} = Wx^{(i)}\).

(This also assumes the data has zero mean — \(p_s = g'\) is symmetric, so it implies \(\mathbb{E}[s]=0\) and hence \(\mathbb{E}[x]=0\). Acoustic signals give you that for free.)

5The assumption that's false

Writing \(\ell(W) = \sum_i \log p(x^{(i)};W)\) quietly assumed the \(x^{(i)}\) are independent across \(i\).

Two different independencesAcross coordinates (\(p(s) = \prod_j p_s(s_j)\)) is the modelling assumption ICA is built on. Across examples is a convenience in writing the likelihood. Don't conflate them.

For speech that's plainly untrue. Consecutive audio samples are heavily correlated — that's what makes it a sound and not static. So the likelihood is misspecified from the start.

In practice it survives: with enough data, correlated training examples don't hurt the estimate much. One cheap trick helps — visit the examples in randomly permuted order when running SGA, so consecutive updates aren't nearly identical. Shuffle the training set.

The honest fix is a model that represents time instead of ignoring it: hidden Markov models, autoregressive models. ICA's version of independence is across speakers; it has nothing to say about across-time structure, and pretending otherwise is a choice you should make knowingly.

The inversion

Nearly everything unsupervised in this course either assumed Gaussianity or was indifferent to it. GDA, mixtures of Gaussians, factor analysis — all built on it. PCA doesn't need it but doesn't benefit either; it only looks at second moments regardless.

ICA is the first method that requires Gaussianity's absence. The rotational symmetry that makes Gaussians so pleasant everywhere else is exactly what destroys identifiability here, and the departures from that symmetry — the corners, the heavy tails, the structure past the covariance — are the only thing telling you where the sources actually point.
