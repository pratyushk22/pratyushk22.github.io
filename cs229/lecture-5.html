Lecture 5

Spend data, spend compute, or spend belief

Last lecture proved the bias–variance tradeoff exists. It said nothing about where to sit on it. This one is three answers, at three prices.

Pratyush · CS229 · cross validation · feature selection · MAP

Suppose you're picking the degree of a polynomial, \(k \in \{0,\dots,10\}\). Or the bandwidth \(\tau\) for locally weighted regression, or \(C\) for the soft-margin SVM. Or just: SVM, neural net, or logistic regression. Call the candidates \(\mathcal{M} = \{M_1,\dots,M_d\}\) — a finite set of models, one of which you have to commit to.

The obvious algorithm, straight out of ERM:

The broken one
  1. Train each \(M_i\) on \(S\), get \(h_i\).
  2. Pick the \(h_i\) with the smallest training error.

Not a bug in the procedureIt's the second term of Lecture 4's bound being invisible. Training error can only see bias. Variance is, by construction, the part it can't measure.

This picks degree 10. Always. Training error is monotonically decreasing in model complexity, so the argmin over complexity is the maximum complexity, every time. The procedure isn't approximately wrong — it reliably selects the highest-variance option available.

The training set fit the model. It cannot also be the judge. Whatever tells you which model to pick has to come from somewhere else — and this lecture is three different somewheres.

1Spend data: cross validation

The cheapest honest fix is to withhold some data from the fitting, and let it do the judging.

Hold-out (simple) cross validation
  1. Split \(S\) randomly into \(S_{\text{train}}\) (~70%) and \(S_{\text{cv}}\) (~30%).
  2. Train each \(M_i\) on \(S_{\text{train}}\) only.
  3. Output the \(h_i\) minimising \(\hat\varepsilon_{S_{\text{cv}}}(h_i)\).

Optional step 3Select \(M_i\), then retrain it on all of \(S\). Usually a good idea — except for algorithms very sensitive to perturbations of the data or initial conditions, where doing well on \(S_{\text{train}}\) doesn't transfer.

Because the models never saw \(S_{\text{cv}}\), \(\hat\varepsilon_{S_{\text{cv}}}\) is an honest estimate of generalization error, and comparing on it is legitimate. Typically a quarter to a third of the data goes into the hold-out set.

And that's the cost, stated plainly: you wasted 30% of your data. Even with the retraining step, every model you compared was trained on \(0.7n\) examples, so you selected a model for a smaller problem than the one you actually have. Fine when data is cheap. Not fine when \(n = 20\).

\(k\)-fold

Hold out less, and rotate which part you hold out.

\(k\)-fold cross validation
  1. Split \(S\) into \(k\) disjoint subsets \(S_1,\dots,S_k\).
  2. For each model \(M_i\): for \(j=1,\dots,k\), train on everything except \(S_j\), test on \(S_j\), and average the \(k\) errors.
  3. Pick the model with the lowest average, retrain on all of \(S\).

\(k=10\) is the usual choice. You now hold out \(1/k\) rather than \(30\%\), so almost all the data trains — but you train each model \(k\) times. You traded data for compute. Push it to the extreme, \(k = n\), and you get leave-one-out: train on \(n-1\), test on the one left over, repeat \(n\) times. Reserved for when data is genuinely scarce, because it's brutally expensive.

One thing that's easy to miss: cross validation isn't only for choosing. It's also just how you honestly estimate how well a single algorithm performs — which is what you'd report in a paper.

2Spend compute: feature selection

A special and important case. You have \(d\) features, \(d\) is enormous, and you suspect only a handful matter. VC dimension scales with \(d\), so the bound from last lecture says you'll overfit unless \(n\) is large. Throwing features away is a direct attack on the variance term.

Each feature is in or out, so there are \(2^d\) subsets — this is model selection over \(2^d\) models. Enumerating them is hopeless, so you search heuristically.

Forward search
  1. \(\mathcal{F} = \emptyset\).
  2. Repeat: for each \(i \notin \mathcal{F}\), evaluate \(\mathcal{F}_i = \mathcal{F}\cup\{i\}\) by cross validation. Set \(\mathcal{F}\) to the best one found.
  3. Output the best subset seen anywhere in the search.

Greedy: add the single feature that helps most, then the next, until \(\mathcal{F}\) is everything or hits a size cap. Backward search is the mirror image — start with all \(d\) features and delete one at a time.

Wrapper methodsThey wrap around your learner and call it repeatedly. That's why they're expensive — and why they're accurate. You're measuring the thing you care about instead of a proxy for it.

Both are wrapper methods: the search doesn't reason about features at all, it just re-runs the whole learning algorithm on each candidate subset and looks at the score. A complete forward search costs about \(O(d^2)\) calls to the learner, each of which is itself a cross validation. It works well and it is very slow.

Filter methods

The cheap alternative: score every feature once, independently, and keep the top \(k\). No retraining, no search — one pass. You're replacing "does this subset actually help my model" with a proxy for "does this feature look informative."

Simplest score: \(|{\rm corr}(x_i, y)\) on the training data. More common for discrete features, mutual information:

\[ \mathrm{MI}(x_i, y) = \sum_{x_i}\sum_{y} p(x_i, y)\log\frac{p(x_i,y)}{p(x_i)p(y)} \]

with all three probabilities estimated from empirical counts. The intuition is much clearer in the other form:

\[ \mathrm{MI}(x_i, y) = \mathrm{KL}\big(p(x_i,y)\;\|\;p(x_i)p(y)\big) \]

What MI is measuringDistance from independence. If \(x_i\) told you nothing about \(y\), the joint would factorise — so measure how badly it fails to.

\(p(x_i)p(y)\) is what the joint distribution would be if the feature and the label were independent. MI is how far the real joint sits from that. Independent \(\Rightarrow\) they're equal \(\Rightarrow\) MI \(=0\) \(\Rightarrow\) the feature is useless, exactly as it should score. Informative features have a joint far from the factorised one, and score high.

Then: how many features do you keep? Choose \(k\) by cross validation. Every road leads back to §1.

3Spend belief: the prior

The third answer doesn't hold out data or search over models. It changes what you're optimising.

Recall MLE:

\[ \theta_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};\theta) \]

This is frequentist. \(\theta\) is a fixed constant of the world — unknown, but not random — and your job is to build a procedure that estimates it. The semicolon in \(p(y\mid x;\theta)\) is doing real work: \(\theta\) parameterises the distribution, it isn't an event you can condition on.

The Bayesian move is to treat \(\theta\) itself as a random variable with a prior \(p(\theta)\) encoding what you believed before seeing data. Then the data updates it:

\[ p(\theta\mid S) = \frac{p(S\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{p(S)} = \frac{\left(\prod_i p(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)},\theta)\right)p(\theta)}{\int_\theta \left(\prod_i p(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)},\theta)\right)p(\theta)\,d\theta} \]

and predictions average over everything \(\theta\) could be, weighted by how plausible the data made it:

\[ p(y\mid x, S) = \int_\theta p(y\mid x,\theta)\,p(\theta\mid S)\,d\theta \]

That's fully Bayesian prediction, and it's usually computationally hopeless — the denominator is an integral over a high-dimensional \(\theta\) with no closed form.

MAP: collapse the posterior to a point

So approximate. Instead of carrying the whole posterior, take its mode:

\[ \theta_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)},\theta)\,p(\theta) \]

Identical to MLE except for one extra factor. The normaliser \(p(S)\) doesn't depend on \(\theta\), so it drops out of the argmax entirely — the intractable integral never has to be computed.

Now take the standard choice \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2 I)\) and put it in log space:

\[ \log p(\theta) = -\frac{\lVert\theta\rVert^2}{2\tau^2} + \text{const} \] \[ \theta_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)},\theta) \;-\; \frac{\lVert\theta\rVert^2}{2\tau^2}\right] \]

The identity worth keepingMAP = MLE + log prior = regularised MLE. \(L_2\) isn't a hack bolted onto the loss; it's a Gaussian prior, written down.

And there it is. A Gaussian prior is an \(L_2\) penalty, with \(\lambda = 1/2\tau^2\). The prior's variance and the regularisation strength are the same knob: small \(\tau\) means you strongly believe \(\theta\) is near zero, which means a heavy penalty on large \(\theta\).

The consequence is that \(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}\) has smaller norm than \(\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}\), and is correspondingly less prone to overfitting. Bayesian logistic regression works well on text classification precisely where you'd expect it to fail — \(d \gg n\), far more features than examples.

Three prices for the same thing

All three sections answer one question — where does the information come from, if not the training error? — and they differ only in what they charge you.

Cross validation spends data: hold some back and let it judge. Feature selection spends compute: re-run the learner over a search space until something wins. The prior spends belief: assert up front that small \(\theta\) is more likely, and let that assertion pay part of the bill.

The last one is the cheapest to run and the most honest about being an assumption. It's also the only one that doesn't need a second dataset — the belief is the second dataset.
