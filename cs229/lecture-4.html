Lecture 4

Permission to trust the training set

Two lemmas, one theorem, and the moment bias and variance stop being vibes and become two terms on a page.

Pratyush · CS229 · learning theory · ERM · VC dimension

Every algorithm so far minimises error on the training set. Nobody has explained why that's allowed. The training set is a finite pile of examples you happened to collect; what you actually want is to be right on the ones you'll see tomorrow. The gap between those two things is generalization error, and this lecture is about earning the right to ignore it.

1Two ways to fail

Fit a line to curved data and it stays wrong no matter how much data you pour in. That's bias — and note the definition, because it's sharper than the usual hand-waving: bias is the generalization error you'd still have after fitting to an infinitely large training set. It's a limitation of the model class, not of the sample.

Fit a 5th-order polynomial to the same data and it passes through every point, including the noise. One house happened to be expensive, another happened to be cheap, and the curve dutifully bends to honour both. That's variance: you learned the sample instead of the distribution.

Both ends are bad, and both are bad in the same currency. So the question becomes: can this picture be made into something you can compute with?

2Everything is built from two lemmas

That's the whole toolkitThe finite-\(\mathcal{H}\) theory needs nothing else. Concentration for one hypothesis, union bound to cover all of them.

Union bound. For events \(A_1,\dots,A_k\), independent or not,

\[ P(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_k) \le P(A_1) + \cdots + P(A_k). \]

Hoeffding (Chernoff). Let \(Z_1,\dots,Z_n\) be iid Bernoulli(\(\varphi\)), and \(\hat\varphi = \frac1n\sum_i Z_i\). Then for any \(\gamma>0\),

\[ P\!\left(\lvert \varphi - \hat\varphi \rvert > \gamma\right) \le 2\exp(-2\gamma^2 n). \]

Toss a biased coin \(n\) times; the observed fraction of heads is close to the true bias with probability that decays exponentially in \(n\). That exponential is the engine of the whole lecture.

3The setup

Binary labels, \(y\in\{0,1\}\). Training set \(S\) of size \(n\) drawn iid from a distribution \(\mathcal{D}\), and — this is the assumption everything rests on — the test examples come from that same \(\mathcal{D}\). Then:

\[ \hat\varepsilon(h) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}\{h(x^{(i)}) \ne y^{(i)}\}, \qquad \varepsilon(h) = P_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big(h(x)\ne y\big) \]

Training error is a fraction you can count. Generalization error is a probability you can never observe. The algorithm we study is empirical risk minimisation — pick whatever minimises the thing you can see:

\[ \hat h = \arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}} \hat\varepsilon(h) \]

where \(\mathcal{H}\) is the hypothesis class, the set of all classifiers the algorithm is willing to consider. For linear classification \(\mathcal{H} = \{h_\theta : h_\theta(x) = \mathbb{1}\{\theta^Tx \ge 0\}\}\). Abstracting to \(\mathcal{H}\) is what lets the argument apply to linear models and neural nets alike.

4Finite \(\mathcal{H}\): from one hypothesis to all of them

Say \(\mathcal{H} = \{h_1,\dots,h_k\}\), \(k\) classifiers, that's it.

Step one — fix a single \(h_i\). Define \(Z_j = \mathbb{1}\{h_i(x^{(j)}) \ne y^{(j)}\}\). These are iid Bernoulli with mean exactly \(\varepsilon(h_i)\), and their sample mean is exactly \(\hat\varepsilon(h_i)\). So Hoeffding applies verbatim:

\[ P\!\left(\lvert \varepsilon(h_i) - \hat\varepsilon(h_i)\rvert > \gamma\right) \le 2\exp(-2\gamma^2 n) \]

Training error is a good estimate of generalization error — for that one hypothesis, fixed in advance.

Step two — but we search. ERM doesn't use a hypothesis fixed in advance; it hunts through all \(k\) for the one that looks best on this sample. So we need the estimate to be reliable for every hypothesis simultaneously, or the winner might just be the luckiest. Union bound over the \(k\) bad events:

\[ P\big(\exists h \in \mathcal{H} : \lvert \varepsilon(h)-\hat\varepsilon(h)\rvert > \gamma\big) \le 2k\exp(-2\gamma^2 n) \]

Flip it: with probability at least \(1 - 2k\exp(-2\gamma^2 n)\), every \(h\in\mathcal{H}\) has its training error within \(\gamma\) of its true error. That's uniform convergence.

Three knobs

Logarithmic in \(k\)Doubling the hypothesis class costs one extra example, near enough. This is why the finite bound isn't as useless as \(k\) being astronomically large would suggest.

There are three quantities — \(n\), \(\gamma\), and the failure probability \(\delta\) — and fixing any two pins the third. Set \(\delta = 2k\exp(-2\gamma^2n)\) and solve for \(n\):

\[ n \ge \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\log\frac{2k}{\delta} \]

This is the sample complexity: how much data you need to buy a guarantee. And the shape matters more than the constants — the dependence on \(k\) is logarithmic. Or solve for \(\gamma\) instead, holding \(n\) and \(\delta\):

\[ \lvert \hat\varepsilon(h) - \varepsilon(h)\rvert \le \sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log\frac{2k}{\delta}} \quad \text{for all } h\in\mathcal{H} \]

5The three lines

Uniform convergence is about estimates being accurate. It doesn't yet say ERM chooses well. Let \(h^\ast = \arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\varepsilon(h)\) be the genuinely best hypothesis in the class — the thing we'd pick if we were omniscient. Then:

\[ \varepsilon(\hat h) \;\le\; \hat\varepsilon(\hat h) + \gamma \;\le\; \hat\varepsilon(h^\ast) + \gamma \;\le\; \varepsilon(h^\ast) + 2\gamma \]

uniform convergence · \(\hat h\) minimises \(\hat\varepsilon\) · uniform convergence again

The middle stepThe only place ERM's definition is used. It's one line, and it's the entire justification for minimising training error.

Read the middle inequality slowly, because it's the one doing the work. \(\hat h\) was chosen to minimise \(\hat\varepsilon\), so \(\hat\varepsilon(\hat h) \le \hat\varepsilon(h^\ast)\) — no probability involved, just the definition of argmin. The outer two steps convert between \(\hat\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon\), paying \(\gamma\) each time.

ERM is at most \(2\gamma\) worse than the best hypothesis the class contains. Not the best possible classifier — the best available one. \(\mathcal{H}\) is a ceiling nobody can raise from inside.

Theorem (finite \(\mathcal{H}\))

With \(\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert = k\) and probability at least \(1-\delta\),

\[ \varepsilon(\hat h) \;\le\; \left(\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\varepsilon(h)\right) + 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log\frac{2k}{\delta}} \]

And there's the tradeoff

Now enlarge the class, \(\mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}' \supseteq \mathcal{H}\), and watch the two terms move in opposite directions.

The first term, \(\min_h \varepsilon(h)\), is a minimum over a larger set — it can only decrease. That's bias falling. The second term grows with \(k\) — it can only increase. That's variance rising.

Bias and variance aren't two folk intuitions that happen to trade off. They're the two summands of one bound, and switching to a richer model class moves both, always in opposite directions. That's the whole tradeoff, on one line.

6Infinite \(\mathcal{H}\)

All of that assumed \(k\) is finite. Linear classifiers are parameterised by real numbers, so \(k = \infty\) and \(\log k\) blows up. Every guarantee dies.

The cheap fix — and it's worth doing precisely because it's wrong in an instructive way: a computer stores a double in 64 bits, so a model with \(d\) parameters really has at most \(k = 2^{64d}\) distinct hypotheses. Plug in:

\[ n \ge O\!\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^2}\log\frac{2^{64d}}{\delta}\right) = O_{\gamma,\delta}(d) \]

Why it's unsatisfyingNot the floating point — the parameterisation. The same class of linear classifiers can be written with \(d+1\) parameters or with \(2d+2\), by substituting \(\theta_i = u_i^2 - v_i^2\). A bound that depends on how you wrote the model down isn't measuring the model.

Sample complexity is linear in the number of parameters. Which is the right answer, arrived at illegitimately.

Shattering and VC dimension

The honest version measures the class by what it can do, not how it's written. Say \(\mathcal{H}\) shatters a set \(S = \{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(D)}\}\) if for every one of the \(2^D\) possible labellings, some \(h\in\mathcal{H}\) realises it. Perfect flexibility on those points.

The VC dimension \(\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})\) is the size of the largest set \(\mathcal{H}\) can shatter.

The subtlety worth flagging: VC dimension is defined by the best set, not all sets. Three collinear points with alternating labels defeat every line — irrelevant. One shatterable set of size \(D\) is all it takes to prove \(\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H}) \ge D\).

Theorem (Vapnik)

Let \(D = \mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})\). With probability at least \(1-\delta\), for all \(h\in\mathcal{H}\),

\[ \lvert \varepsilon(h) - \hat\varepsilon(h)\rvert \le O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{D}{n}\log\frac{n}{D} + \frac1n\log\frac1\delta}\right) \]

and hence \(\varepsilon(\hat h) \le \varepsilon(h^\ast) + O(\cdot)\), with \(n = O_{\gamma,\delta}(D)\) sufficing.

Same shape as before, with \(\log k\) replaced by \(D\). Finite VC dimension is exactly the condition for uniform convergence to kick in as \(n\) grows — and \(D\) is a property of the function class itself, immune to how you chose to parameterise it.

What this bought

For most reasonable classes, VC dimension is roughly the number of parameters — so the honest argument lands in the same place as the 64-bit hack, without the embarrassment. Data needed \(\approx\) linear in parameters.

Two caveats I don't want to lose. All of this was proved for ERM; algorithms that don't minimise training error (or an approximation of it) aren't covered. And every line depended on train and test coming from the same \(\mathcal{D}\) — break that, and the theorems say nothing at all.
