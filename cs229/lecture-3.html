Lecture 3

Never write down the features

Kernels and SVMs, read as one idea: stop storing the thing you think you're learning, and store the coefficients that reconstruct it.

Pratyush · CS229 · kernels · duality · SVM · SMO

Linear regression fits \(\theta^T x\). If the truth is a cubic, that's useless — but only if you insist that \(x\) is the thing you regress on. Write

\[ \phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x \\ x^2 \\ x^3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^4, \qquad \theta_3 x^3 + \theta_2 x^2 + \theta_1 x + \theta_0 = \theta^T \phi(x) \]

and the cubic is linear — in \(\phi(x)\). The raw input \(x\) is the attribute, \(\phi(x)\) is the feature, and \(\phi : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^p\) is the feature map. Nothing about the algorithm changes; every \(x^{(i)}\) in the LMS update just becomes \(\phi(x^{(i)})\):

\[ \theta := \theta + \alpha \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y^{(i)} - \theta^T\phi(x^{(i)})\right)\phi(x^{(i)}) \]

So expressiveness is free. Except it isn't, and the rest of the lecture is the bill.

1The bill

Take \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and let \(\phi(x)\) hold every monomial of degree \(\le 3\). Then \(p \sim d^3\). At \(d = 1000\) that's a \(10^9\)-dimensional vector you have to compute, store, and update every single step. Six orders of magnitude worse than plain least squares.

And the reason looks unavoidable: \(\theta\) itself lives in \(\mathbb{R}^p\). If the parameter has \(10^9\) entries, surely you have to keep \(10^9\) numbers.

Unless you don't store \(\theta\).

2Store \(\beta\), not \(\theta\)

Initialise \(\theta = 0\) and watch what the update rule can and cannot produce. At step zero, \(\theta = \sum_i 0\cdot\phi(x^{(i)})\). Suppose at some point

\[ \theta = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_i \phi(x^{(i)}). \]

Not an assumptionThis is proved by induction from the update rule plus \(\theta=0\). Every increment is a combination of the \(\phi(x^{(i)})\), so \(\theta\) can never leave their span.

The next update adds \(\alpha\sum_i (y^{(i)} - \theta^T\phi(x^{(i)}))\,\phi(x^{(i)})\) — which is, again, a linear combination of exactly the same \(n\) vectors. So the form survives, with

\[ \beta_i := \beta_i + \alpha\left(y^{(i)} - \theta^T\phi(x^{(i)})\right). \]

\(\theta\) is confined to the span of the training features, forever. Gradient descent never had the ability to step outside it. So instead of \(p\) numbers, keep \(n\) numbers — the coefficients \(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n\) — and treat them as the parameters.

When this actually helpsOnly when \(p \gg n\). If you have more data than features, \(\beta\) is the bigger object and the "confinement" buys you nothing.

Worth being honest about what this is. The primal stores \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p\); the dual stores \(\beta \in \mathbb{R}^n\). It's a change of what you write down, not a change of model — the same function is being fit either way. It's a win precisely when the feature space is huge and the dataset is small.

The inner product falls out

Substituting \(\theta = \sum_j \beta_j \phi(x^{(j)})\) into the \(\beta\) update:

\[ \beta_i := \beta_i + \alpha\left(y^{(i)} - \sum_{j=1}^{n} \big\langle \phi(x^{(j)}), \phi(x^{(i)}) \big\rangle \beta_j \right) \]

Look at what survives. \(\phi\) appears only inside an inner product. Never alone, never as a coordinate you have to read. That's the opening.

Because for the degree-\(\le 3\) map, that inner product collapses:

\[ \langle \phi(x),\phi(z)\rangle = 1 + \langle x,z\rangle + \langle x,z\rangle^2 + \langle x,z\rangle^3 \]

Compute \(\langle x,z\rangle\) in \(O(d)\), then a constant number of multiplications. \(O(d)\) instead of \(O(d^3)\), and \(\phi(x)\) is never built. Define

\[ K(x,z) \triangleq \langle \phi(x), \phi(z)\rangle \]
Kernelised LMS
  1. Precompute \(K_{ij} = K(x^{(i)}, x^{(j)})\) for all \(i,j\). Set \(\beta := 0\).
  2. Loop: \(\;\beta := \beta + \alpha(\vec{y} - K\beta)\)
  3. Predict: \(\;\theta^T\phi(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_i K(x^{(i)}, x)\)

\(O(n)\) per update once \(K\) is built, and \(O(n^2 d)\) to build it. The prediction rule is the tell: to score a new point you never touch \(\theta\), you just ask how similar \(x\) is to each training point.

3What makes a kernel a kernel

Now flip the interface. The algorithm above never mentions \(\phi\). So why start from \(\phi\) at all? Just pick a function \(K\) directly — you only need \(\phi\) to exist, not to be writable.

The second reading of \(K\) makes this feel natural: an inner product is a similarity measure. \(K(x,z)\) is large when the two points look alike in feature space, near zero when they're nearly orthogonal. So design similarity, not coordinates. The Gaussian / RBF kernel

\[ K(x,z) = \exp\left(-\frac{\lVert x-z\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

is exactly this: 1 when \(x=z\), decaying to 0 as they separate. And the \(\phi\) behind it is infinite-dimensional — a thing you could not have written down even in principle. You get it anyway.

Mercer's condition

So which \(K\) are legal? Build the kernel matrix \(K_{ij} = K(x^{(i)},x^{(j)})\) on any finite set of points. If \(K\) is valid, then \(K_{ij} = \phi(x^{(i)})^T\phi(x^{(j)}) = K_{ji}\), so it's symmetric. And for any \(z\),

\[ z^T K z = \sum_k \left(\sum_i z_i \phi_k(x^{(i)})\right)^{\!2} \;\ge\; 0 \]

Necessary and sufficientRare and useful. Symmetric + PSD isn't just implied by validity — it's a complete characterisation. Check it, and \(\phi\) is guaranteed to exist.

so it's positive semi-definite. Those are the necessary conditions, and Mercer's theorem says they're also sufficient: symmetric PSD kernel matrix for every finite set \(\iff\) some \(\phi\) exists.

You never have to find \(\phi\). You only have to check two properties of a matrix.

And the general lesson is bigger than regression: any algorithm you can write using only inner products \(\langle x,z\rangle\) can be kernelised by swapping in \(K(x,z)\). Perceptron, PCA, and the one this was really built for —

4SVMs: the geometric one

Everything so far was algebra about storage. SVMs are the one algorithm in the course where the driving idea is genuinely geometric: don't just separate the classes, separate them with as much clearance as you can.

The intuition is confidence. A point sitting far from the decision boundary is one you'd bet on; a point hugging the boundary could flip under a tiny perturbation of \(w\). So maximise the distance from the boundary to the nearest point. And this is where the kernel comes back in: climb into higher dimensions and separability gets easier — more room, more directions along which the classes can come apart.

Functional vs geometric margin

Switch to \(y \in \{-1,1\}\) and \(h_{w,b}(x) = g(w^Tx+b)\), splitting the intercept out. The functional margin of an example is

\[ \hat\gamma^{(i)} = y^{(i)}\left(w^Tx^{(i)} + b\right) \]

Positive means correct, large means confident. Except it's a fake measure — and this is the bit worth internalising. \(g\) only reads the sign of \(w^Tx+b\), so replacing \((w,b)\) with \((cw,cb)\) for \(c>0\) changes nothing about the classifier but multiplies the margin by \(c\). You can inflate your own confidence by rescaling. That's cheating.

FixNormalise: \(w \to w/\lVert w\rVert\), \(b \to b/\lVert w\rVert\). The geometric margin is scale-invariant because the cheat divides out.

The geometric margin divides it out — it's the actual perpendicular distance to the hyperplane:

\[ \gamma^{(i)} = y^{(i)}\left(\left(\frac{w}{\lVert w\rVert}\right)^{\!T} x^{(i)} + \frac{b}{\lVert w\rVert}\right), \qquad \gamma = \min_i \gamma^{(i)} \]

Maximise \(\gamma\) subject to every point clearing it, plus \(\lVert w\rVert = 1\). Correct, and unsolvable — that norm constraint is non-convex.

The escape uses the cheat as a tool rather than fighting it. Since scaling is free, just spend it: demand \(\hat\gamma = 1\). Then \(\gamma = 1/\lVert w\rVert\), maximising that is minimising \(\lVert w\rVert^2\), and:

\[ \min_{w,b} \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y^{(i)}(w^Tx^{(i)}+b) \ge 1 \]

Convex quadratic objective, linear constraints. Solvable. The degree of freedom that made the functional margin meaningless is exactly the degree of freedom that buys convexity.

The dual, again

We could stop here and hand it to a QP solver. We don't, and the reason is the same as in §2: we want the \(n\)-sized description, not the \(d\)-sized one, because that's where the kernel plugs in.

Form the Lagrangian, minimise over \(w,b\), and you get \(w = \sum_i \alpha_i y^{(i)} x^{(i)}\) — \(w\) is spanned by the data, same story as \(\theta = \sum\beta_i\phi(x^{(i)})\). Substitute back:

\[ \max_\alpha \; W(\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j=1}^{n} y^{(i)}y^{(j)}\alpha_i\alpha_j \langle x^{(i)}, x^{(j)}\rangle \] \[ \text{s.t.} \quad \alpha_i \ge 0, \qquad \sum_i \alpha_i y^{(i)} = 0 \]

The data enters only through \(\langle x^{(i)},x^{(j)}\rangle\). Swap in \(K\) and you're running an SVM in an infinite-dimensional space at finite cost. That was the whole point of the detour.

Two facts about duality that are worth keeping separate. Weak duality always holds: \(\max\min L \le \min\max L\), for any problem, no conditions. Strong duality (\(d^\ast = p^\ast\)) needs convexity, affine equality constraints, and feasibility — which our problem satisfies. Then the solution is characterised by KKT:

  • Primal feasibility the original constraints hold
  • Dual feasibility \(\alpha_i \ge 0\)
  • Stationarity \(\nabla_w L = 0\)
  • Complementary slackness \(\alpha_i \, g_i(w) = 0\)

Where sparsity comes fromNot a regulariser, not a design choice. It's forced by one KKT equation.

That last one is the payoff. \(\alpha_i g_i(w) = 0\) means: either the constraint is tight, or \(\alpha_i = 0\). A point that isn't sitting exactly on the margin has \(\alpha_i = 0\) and contributes nothing. The surviving handful are the support vectors, and

\[ w^Tx + b = \sum_{i \in SV} \alpha_i y^{(i)} \langle x^{(i)}, x\rangle + b \]

Prediction touches only those. The boundary is determined by the points that were hardest to classify — and everything comfortably correct is discarded.

SMO

Last piece: how to actually maximise \(W(\alpha)\). Coordinate ascent is the obvious move — fix everything, optimise one \(\alpha_i\). It fails immediately, and for a nice reason: \(\sum_i \alpha_i y^{(i)} = 0\) means fixing \(n-1\) multipliers determines the last one. There's no freedom left to move.

So move two at a time. That's SMO:

SMO
  1. Pick the pair \(\alpha_i, \alpha_j\) that most violates KKT — the two worst offenders.
  2. Reoptimise \(W\) over those two, holding the rest fixed. The equality constraint pins them to a line, \(\alpha_i y^{(i)} + \alpha_j y^{(j)} = \zeta\), so it's a one-variable quadratic. Solve in closed form.
  3. Box constraints force \((\alpha_i,\alpha_j)\) into \([0,C]\times[0,C]\). If the unconstrained optimum falls outside, clip it to the allowed interval.

(The \(C\) is the soft-margin knob from \(\ell_1\) regularisation: it lets points sit inside the margin at a price, so the whole thing survives non-separable data and doesn't swing wildly for one outlier. Curiously, the only change it makes to the dual is \(0 \le \alpha_i\) becoming \(0 \le \alpha_i \le C\).)

Line constraint, box constraint, clip. Two-dimensional geometry, done thousands of times.

The one line I'd keep

Both halves of this lecture are the same move. In kernel LMS, \(\theta\) is trapped in the span of \(\phi(x^{(i)})\), so store \(\beta \in \mathbb{R}^n\). In the SVM, \(w\) is trapped in the span of \(x^{(i)}\), so store \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^n\). Both rewrites leave the data appearing only inside inner products, and that is what lets \(K\) walk in and hand you a feature space you could never have built.

The kernel trick isn't a trick about kernels. It's a trick about what you refuse to write down.
