Lecture 8

The bound that touches

Last time EM was a plausible heuristic with two unpaid debts. Jensen's inequality settles both.

Pratyush · CS229 · Jensen · ELBO · EM · VAE

Lecture 7 ended owing two things. The E-step was proposed, never derived — why the posterior and not some other guess? And unlike k-means, which had a distortion function decreasing monotonically as proof, EM had no object guaranteeing it improves anything at all.

Both debts are paid by one inequality.

1Jensen

For convex \(f\) and any random variable \(X\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \]

and if \(f\) is strictly convex, equality holds if and only if \(X\) is constant with probability 1.

Keep the equality conditionEveryone remembers the inequality and forgets that equality requires a constant. That condition is what derives the E-step. It isn't a footnote.

And \(\log\) is concave (\(f''(x) = -1/x^2 < 0\)), so for \(\log\) every inequality flips: \(\mathbb{E}[\log X] \le \log \mathbb{E}[X]\). That direction is the one we want — it turns something we can't compute into a lower bound we can.

2The obstruction, restated

With latent \(z\), the marginal is \(p(x;\theta) = \sum_z p(x,z;\theta)\), so

\[ \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(x^{(i)};\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sum_{z^{(i)}} p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)};\theta) \]

Sum inside the log. Nothing separates, no closed form, and the objective is non-concave — so even a working algorithm reaches a local optimum determined by where you initialised. That last part never goes away; everything below is about getting somewhere reliably, not about getting to the global maximum.

3Build a bound

Take one example. Let \(Q\) be any distribution over \(z\) (\(\sum_z Q(z) = 1\), \(Q(z)\ge0\)). Multiply and divide:

\[ \log p(x;\theta) = \log\sum_z p(x,z;\theta) = \log\sum_z Q(z)\,\frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)} = \log\;\mathbb{E}_{z\sim Q}\!\left[\frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)}\right] \]

That's a log of an expectation. Jensen for concave \(f\) pushes the log inside and pays for it with an inequality:

\[ \log p(x;\theta) \;\ge\; \sum_z Q(z)\log\frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)} \;\triangleq\; \mathrm{ELBO}(x;Q,\theta) \]

The evidence lower bound. And read the quantifier carefully, because it's the useful part: this holds for every \(Q\) and every \(\theta\). You haven't got one bound, you've got a whole family of them, indexed by \(Q\).

Which one?

A lower bound is only useful if it's close. Push it as high as it goes — make it touch. Equality in Jensen requires the random variable inside to be constant:

\[ \frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)} = c \quad\text{independent of } z \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q(z) \propto p(x,z;\theta) \]

and since \(Q\) must sum to 1, the normaliser is forced:

\[ Q(z) = \frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{\sum_z p(x,z;\theta)} = \frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{p(x;\theta)} = p(z\mid x;\theta) \]

Debt one, paidThe posterior isn't a clever guess. It's the unique \(Q\) making Jensen tight, and it drops out of the equality condition with no choices made.

The E-step is not a design decision. It is what "make the bound tight" evaluates to.

Verify it directly if you want — substitute \(Q(z) = p(z|x;\theta)\), use \(p(x,z) = p(z|x)p(x)\), and the \(p(z|x)\) cancels:

\[ \sum_z p(z|x;\theta)\log\frac{p(z|x;\theta)\,p(x;\theta)}{p(z|x;\theta)} = \log p(x;\theta)\sum_z p(z|x;\theta) = \log p(x;\theta) \]

4The algorithm

Back to \(n\) examples. The optimal \(Q\) depends on the example, so carry \(n\) of them, \(Q_1,\dots,Q_n\):

\[ \ell(\theta) \;\ge\; \sum_i \mathrm{ELBO}(x^{(i)};Q_i,\theta) = \sum_i\sum_{z^{(i)}} Q_i(z^{(i)})\log\frac{p(x^{(i)},z^{(i)};\theta)}{Q_i(z^{(i)})} \]
EM, general form

E-step. For each \(i\), set \(\;Q_i(z^{(i)}) := p(z^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};\theta)\).

M-step. Set \(\;\theta := \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{ELBO}(x^{(i)};Q_i,\theta)\).

Coordinate ascent, againSame shape as k-means being coordinate descent on \(J\). Two blocks of variables, alternate exact maximisation over each.

Which reframes the whole thing. Define \(\mathrm{ELBO}(Q,\theta) = \sum_i \mathrm{ELBO}(x^{(i)};Q_i,\theta)\), a function of two arguments. Then EM is alternating maximisation of one objective: the E-step maximises it over \(Q\), the M-step over \(\theta\). It stops looking like a two-part heuristic and starts looking like coordinate ascent, which is what it is.

5Why it can't go down

Let \(\theta^{(t)}\), \(\theta^{(t+1)}\) be successive iterates, with \(Q_i^{(t)}(z^{(i)}) = p(z^{(i)}|x^{(i)};\theta^{(t)})\) from the E-step. Then:

\[ \ell(\theta^{(t)}) = \sum_i \mathrm{ELBO}(x^{(i)};Q_i^{(t)},\theta^{(t)}) \] \[ \ell(\theta^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \sum_i \mathrm{ELBO}(x^{(i)};Q_i^{(t)},\theta^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \sum_i \mathrm{ELBO}(x^{(i)};Q_i^{(t)},\theta^{(t)}) \;=\; \ell(\theta^{(t)}) \]

the bound holds for all \(Q,\theta\) · \(\theta^{(t+1)}\) was chosen as the argmax · equality from the E-step

\(\ell(\theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \ell(\theta^{(t)})\). Monotone improvement. Debt two, paid.

Where tightness earns its keepWithout the touch, the bound could peak somewhere \(\ell\) is lower. Climbing a loose bound proves nothing.

The picture is worth more than the algebra. You have a hard function \(\ell\) and an easy bound sitting under it. Make the bound touch at where you are, jump to the bound's maximum, and you're guaranteed not to have gone down — because \(\ell\) is above the bound at the new point, and the bound went up.

Note what's not claimed. Monotone increase, bounded above, so \(\ell\) converges. Nothing says the limit is the global maximum — the objective is non-concave and always was. Same caveat as k-means, same fix: reinitialise, keep the best.

6Two other faces of the ELBO

Split the log:

\[ \mathrm{ELBO}(x;Q,\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim Q}[\log p(x\mid z;\theta)] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(Q \,\|\, p_z\big) \]

Reconstruction minus a penalty for straying from the prior over \(z\). And when \(p_z\) doesn't depend on \(\theta\) — usually true — maximising the ELBO over \(\theta\) is just maximising \(\mathbb{E}_Q[\log p(x|z;\theta)]\): the conditional likelihood, which is the easy problem we always wished we had.

The other rearrangement is the one I'd frame:

\[ \mathrm{ELBO}(x;Q,\theta) = \log p(x) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(Q\,\|\,p_{z\mid x}\big) \]

\(\log p(x)\) doesn't involve \(Q\). KL is \(\ge 0\) and zero only when the two distributions agree. So maximising the ELBO over \(Q\) means minimising the KL from \(Q\) to the true posterior, and the maximum is at \(Q = p_{z|x}\) — the E-step, derived a second time, in one line. It also makes the gap explicit: the slack between \(\ell\) and the bound is that KL divergence.

7When the posterior is out of reach

For mixtures of Gaussians the E-step was a Bayes-rule calculation you could write down. Now take

\[ z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{k\times k}), \qquad x\mid z \sim \mathcal{N}\big(g(z;\theta),\,\sigma^2 I\big) \]

with \(g\) a neural network. The posterior \(p(z|x;\theta)\) is intractable. The E-step is unavailable.

But look again at what §6 said: the E-step is minimising KL to the posterior. If you can't reach the minimum, get as close as some restricted family lets you. Pick a family \(\mathcal{Q}\) and solve

\[ \max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\;\max_\theta\; \mathrm{ELBO}(Q,\theta) \]

The bound is still a bound — that was true for any \(Q\). You just don't get to make it touch anymore. That's variational inference: trade exactness for feasibility, and know exactly what you gave up (the residual KL).

The VAE's choice of family: a Gaussian per example, with mean and variance produced by networks reading \(x^{(i)}\),

\[ Q_i = \mathcal{N}\big(q(x^{(i)};\phi),\; \mathrm{diag}(v(x^{(i)};\psi))^2\big) \]

What the prior actually is\(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) isn't a claim that the data's latent structure is Gaussian. It's a choice of coordinate system for the latent space — a convenient one to sample from and take KL against.

with \(q,v\) the encoder and \(g(z;\theta)\) the decoder. These \(Q_i\) are often a poor approximation to the true posterior. That's accepted; some approximation is the price of being able to optimise at all.

The reparameterisation trick

Exact alternating maximisation is off the table now, so just do gradient ascent on \(\phi,\psi,\theta\) together. \(\nabla_\theta\) is easy — \(\theta\) sits inside the expectation, so the gradient passes straight in.

\(\nabla_\phi\) is not, and the reason is worth stating precisely: \(\phi\) parameterises the distribution you're sampling from. So

\[ \nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{z\sim Q_\phi}[f] \;\ne\; \mathbb{E}_{z\sim Q_\phi}[\nabla_\phi f] \]

because moving \(\phi\) moves the sampling measure, not just the integrand. The fix is to relocate the randomness so it doesn't depend on \(\phi\) at all. Using \(x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2) \iff x = \mu + \sigma\xi\) with \(\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), write

\[ z^{(i)} = q(x^{(i)};\phi) + v(x^{(i)};\psi)\odot\xi^{(i)}, \qquad \xi^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I) \]

Now the expectation is over \(\xi\), which knows nothing about \(\phi\), and the parameters live in a deterministic function inside. The gradient goes in. Sample a few \(\xi\) (in practice, often one) and you have an estimator.

The pattern

Strip the specifics and the same move appears three times. The thing you want to maximise is intractable, so build a lower bound you can maximise, make it as tight as you're able, climb it, repeat.

EM is the case where "as tight as you're able" means exactly — the posterior is computable, the bound touches, and monotone improvement follows as a theorem. Variational inference is the same procedure with the touch relaxed to whatever a restricted family can manage. You lose the guarantee, and in exchange the method survives contact with neural networks.
